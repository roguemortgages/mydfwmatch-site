Texas property taxes

Texas property taxes, explained.

If you're moving from a state with an income tax, Texas property taxes can catch you off guard. Here's how they actually work, in plain English, so there are no surprises when you see the payment.

Why they're a bigger deal here

Texas doesn't have a state income tax, but the money to run schools, cities, and counties has to come from somewhere, and in Texas a lot of it comes from property taxes. That's why the tax line on a Texas mortgage payment can be as big as (or bigger than) the insurance and HOA lines combined.

Your bill is a stack, not one rate

Every property sits inside several taxing districts at once, and each one adds its own rate:

  • The county. Every home has one.
  • The city. Unless the home is outside city limits.
  • The school district. Usually the biggest slice. And district lines don't follow city lines, so two homes in the same city can land in different districts.
  • Special districts. Hospital districts, community colleges and, in a lot of newer DFW neighborhoods, a MUD or a PID.

Add those rates together and you get the combined rate for that address. So two homes in the same city can have different tax rates, sometimes just a street apart.

What MUDs and PIDs actually are

A MUD (municipal utility district) is a special district that pays for the water, sewer, and drainage in a newer development, then charges homeowners a tax to pay that debt down over time. A PID (public improvement district) works similarly for things like roads, landscaping, or amenities, and shows up as an assessment on the property. Neither one is a red flag. They're super common in DFW's growth areas. Just know the number before you fall in love with the house.

What can bring the bill down

  • The homestead exemption. If the home is your primary residence, you can apply with your county appraisal district to take a chunk off the taxable value for school district taxes. Some cities and counties offer their own on top of that. It isn't automatic, so actually file it after you close.
  • The 10% cap. Once you have a homestead exemption, the taxable value of your home can't go up more than 10% a year (plus any improvements you make), even if the market jumps.
  • Protesting your value. Every spring the appraisal district sends a notice with your home's value. If it looks high, you can protest it, usually by May 15 or 30 days after the notice, whichever is later. It's free.

Exemption amounts and rules change with the Texas Legislature. For the current numbers, go straight to the Texas Comptroller and your county appraisal district.

The part that trips up new buyers

The tax amount on a listing or a prior year's bill shows what the last owner paid. They may have had a homestead exemption, an over-65 freeze, or a value that was capped for years. When the home sells, the value can reset and your bill can look very different. So when you're comparing homes, ask for an estimate based on your purchase price and the full combined rate for that address.
