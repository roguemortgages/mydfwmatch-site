Method and sources

You should be able to check the answer.

Confident-sounding is easy. Explainable is better. Here's exactly what goes into a match, where the data comes from and what it can't know.

What the model knows: comparable, area-level data for 59 DFW cities and city sections, and one documented scoring framework.

What it can't know: the exact address, today's listings, a specific HOA, the condition of a real home or what traffic looks like on your street at your start time. Those aren't tiny caveats. They're the next round of questions.

What changes the result

  • Your commute. How much the drive matters to you sets how heavily commute time counts. If you said a short commute is the point, areas with a long drive to your job center get set aside entirely. A second regular commute counts too.
  • How you live. Each answer about yard space, home age, errands, walkability, lake access and planned or rural settings nudges a few measures up or down. Answers that point the same direction reinforce each other, up to a limit, so one topic can't drown out everything else.
  • Your “if one thing gets to win” answer. It breaks ties toward what you said matters most.
  • Not your budget, timeline or rent-or-buy answer. Those shape the guidance you get, not the ranking.

The highest score is your best-fit area. If the top two are within half a point, you see it as a close call. If they're exactly even, you get a shortlist instead of a winner I made up.

Where the data comes from

  • Drive times: TomTom historical-traffic forecasts for weekday 7 and 8 AM departures, from two or three public starting points in each area to each of the 13 major job centers. Over 3,000 routes in all.
  • Home age and yard space: U.S. Census Bureau American Community Survey 5-year estimates. Newer homes means built 2010 or later. Move-in ready means built 1980 through 2009. Established character means built before 1980. Yard space looks at homes per square mile of residential land, so airports and lakes don't count as anybody's yard.
  • Errands and walkability: OpenStreetMap data, measured across every residential street inside each area's official boundary. Errands means how far a typical home is from the nearest grocery store or pharmacy. Walkability means the share of homes with at least 10 restaurants, shops or everyday places within a 10-minute walk.
  • Lakes: the Texas Water Development Board's map of existing reservoirs, measured from the same residential streets.
  • Planned communities: documented master-planned communities in each area.

Each measure is compared across the whole metroplex on a 1–5 scale, where 3 is middle-of-the-road for DFW. Data is refreshed on a schedule and every change is logged.

What never goes into a match

Race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, disability or any other protected characteristic. No ratings of residents, schools, crime, safety or whether a neighborhood is “good.” The quiz never asks who lives in your home. Read the fair housing commitment.
